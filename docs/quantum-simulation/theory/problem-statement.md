# Problem Statement


## What Problem Are We Trying to Solve?


The main objective of first-principles calculations is to predict the electronic structure and physical properties of real materials using quantum mechanical approaches.


Materials are composed of atoms, and atoms consist of electrons and nuclei. The behavior of electrons inside a material is governed by the principles of quantum mechanics.


In quantum mechanics, the state of a particle is described by a wave function. The wave function contains information about the probability distribution and physical properties of the particle.



---

## Wave Function and Quantum Description


The relationship between the momentum of a particle and its wavelength is described by the de Broglie equation:


\[
\lambda p = h
\]


where:


- \(\lambda\) is the wavelength of the particle
- \(p\) is the momentum
- \(h\) is Planck's constant



---

## Schrödinger Equation


The electronic behavior of a system is described by solving the Schrödinger equation.


For an electron moving in a potential field \(V(\mathbf{r},t)\):


\[
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)
+
V(\mathbf{r},t)\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)
=
i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t}
\]


where:


- \(\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)\) represents the electron wave function
- \(V(\mathbf{r},t)\) represents the potential energy field
- \(\hbar\) is the reduced Planck constant
- \(m\) is the electron mass



---

## Time Independent Approximation


In many material systems, the potential field changes much slower compared with electron motion.


For example, electrons interact with the Coulomb potential generated by atomic nuclei. Since nuclei are significantly heavier than electrons, their movement can be considered relatively slow.


Therefore, the wave function can be separated into spatial and temporal components:


\[
\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)=\psi(\mathbf{r})f(t)
\]


This approximation transforms the time-dependent Schrödinger equation into the time-independent form:


\[
\left[
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2
+
V(\mathbf{r})
\right]
\psi(\mathbf{r})
=
\epsilon\psi(\mathbf{r})
\]


This equation describes the allowed electronic states and corresponding energy levels of a quantum system.



---

## Physical Observables


Once the wave function is obtained, measurable physical properties can be calculated using expectation values.


The orthogonality condition between electronic states is:


\[
\langle\psi_i|\psi_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}
\]


The energy of an electronic state can be obtained from:


\[
\langle\psi_i|\hat{H}|\psi_i\rangle=\epsilon_i
\]


where \(\hat{H}\) represents the Hamiltonian operator.



---

## The Many-Body Problem


Although the Schrödinger equation provides a complete description of quantum systems, solving it directly for real materials is extremely challenging.


A realistic material contains a large number of interacting electrons. The electronic wave function becomes a many-body function:


\[
\left[
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_i^2
+
\sum_{i=1}^{N}V(r_i)
+
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j<i}U(r_i,r_j)
\right]
\Psi(r_1,r_2,...,r_N)
=
E\Psi(r_1,r_2,...,r_N)
\]


For macroscopic materials, the number of electrons can reach approximately:


\[
N \approx 10^{23}
\]


Solving this complete many-body problem requires computational resources beyond conventional approaches.



---

## From Quantum Mechanics to Computational Materials Science


To overcome the many-body problem, computational materials science develops approximation methods that maintain important physical information while reducing computational complexity.


One of the most successful approaches is Density Functional Theory (DFT).


Instead of solving the full many-electron wave function, DFT uses electron density as the fundamental variable.


DFT enables researchers to study:


- Electronic structure
- Material stability
- Chemical bonding
- Surface properties
- Battery materials
- Catalytic systems
